# 📦 About the Japanese second-hand market

## Introduction

*Apart from the small supplies*, most of this conversion's components **cannot be bought in regular shops**. This is very specific arcade hardware, sold only by the official manufacturers, and going through Sega's after-sales service is obviously impossible. That leaves only person-to-person sales.

**We therefore depend on what is available on the second-hand market.** A Sega IO4 board or an Aime reader is fairly easy to find, an ALLS HX2 much less so, and an official DX touchscreen is practically impossible to find. For some parts, alternatives exist. For the others, you will have to be patient and watch for an offer on the Japanese second-hand market.

!!! tip "Where do the spare parts come from?"
    Most Japanese second-hand parts come from cabinets dismantled to be resold more easily, most often after an arcade closed down or because the cabinet no longer drew players. DX being very popular, its cabinets are rarely exported, and even more rarely taken apart to be sold as spare parts.

    Fortunately, **most Sega components are interchangeable** and reused from one cabinet to another, including on machines far less popular than DX, whose parts are much easier to find.

## Buying through a proxy

Most parts from the [shopping list](equipment.md) can be found on **JDirectItems Auction**, Japan's largest auction site. The catch: like the vast majority of Japanese shopping sites, it requires **a Japanese address, phone number and payment method**. Without living in Japan, you cannot buy there directly.

This is where **proxy buying services** come in: companies with an address and an account in Japan, which bid, buy and receive parcels on your behalf, then forward them to you internationally. They charge a commission and service fees, on top of the item price and shipping costs.

Among these services:

* **[Neokyo]({{NEOKYO}})**
* **[Zenmarket]({{ZENMARKET}})**
* **[FROM JAPAN]({{FROM_JAPAN}})**
* **[Buyee]({{BUYEE}})**: one of the best known and oldest, but read the warning below.

!!! warning "Buyee does not ship some electronic parts"
    Buyee refuses to ship internationally any item containing a lithium cell or battery, under the regulations on air transport of dangerous goods. Yet some of the parts we are after, such as the ALLS HX2, include a small lithium coin cell that powers their internal clock (RTC) when switched off. **In practice, these parts therefore cannot be shipped through Buyee.**

    For these items, prefer a service like Neokyo or Zenmarket, which do not apply this restriction.

The process is similar from one service to another:

1. You create an account.
2. You submit the link to the item you want (or browse JDirectItems Auction directly from their site).
3. You top up your account with the required amount, and the proxy handles the purchase.
4. The proxy receives the item and stores it for you.
    * Most services store items for free for a certain period.
    * You can therefore **group several items** in their warehouse to ship everything in a single parcel.
5. When ready, request packing: the proxy packs your items and ships them to your address outside Japan.

!!! tip "Ask for photos before shipping"
    Once the item reaches their warehouse, you can usually ask for **extra photos** before approving the shipment. This is very useful to check the condition of a second-hand part before it crosses half the globe. You will not be refunded if there is a problem, but you will know right away whether to look for the part elsewhere, without waiting for the parcel to arrive.

## Search tips

Finding the right part on JDirectItems Auction can be frustrating without a few basics:

* **Search with the official part names.** A search in English or French almost never returns relevant results. Use the names and part numbers from the official diagrams and manuals instead (for example `ALLS HX2`, `IO4`, `Aime`), which stay the same in Japanese since they are commercial references. A translator (DeepL, Google Translate) will help you understand a listing's title and description.
* **Don't shy away from "ジャンク" (*junk*).** Working hardware is often sold with this label, simply because it has not been tested recently. It is not necessarily broken: it is just sold "as is", with no guarantee.
* **Check the seller.** The percentage of positive ratings (top right of the listing) is a good reliability indicator. A professional seller used to dismantling arcade cabinets is generally a sign of trustworthiness.
* **Examine the photos.** Japanese sellers are usually very thorough with photos, even without an English description. Zoom in on the connectors, the condition of the pins and the labels or markings that confirm the part's exact reference.
* **Watch the auction end time.** Auctions end on Japanese time (JST, UTC+9), often in the middle of the night in Europe. Most proxies let you place a **maximum automatic bid** in advance: no need to stay up to outbid at the last second.
* **Plan for extra costs.** The displayed price is never the final price. Add:
    * the proxy's commission;
    * shipping to their warehouse;
    * international shipping, often calculated by weight **and** volume;
    * any customs fees on arrival, which vary by country.

    A compact circuit board is usually reasonable to ship, but keep this calculation in mind before bidding too high on a rare part.
* **Set up an alert.** Most proxies let you save a search and get notified as soon as a matching item is listed. This is especially useful for rare parts like the ALLS HX2, which can take weeks, or even months, to reappear.
